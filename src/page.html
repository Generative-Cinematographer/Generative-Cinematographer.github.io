Generative Cinematographer: Composing Camera and Object Motion in 3D

Abstract

Existing controllable video generation methods can model camera motion or object motion, but most operate through image-space controls such as 2D trajectories or sparse drag signals. These controls are fundamentally ambiguous: the same image-plane motion may correspond to multiple underlying 3D motions, making camera motion and object motion difficult to disentangle under viewpoint changes. We present Generative Cinematographer, a controllable video generation framework that lifts a monocular image or video into an editable 4D scene scaffold where camera motion and foreground motion are authored jointly in a shared world frame. Our key observation is that world-space motion can be rasterized into dense correspondence maps that preserve persistent identities across viewpoints while remaining compatible with RGB-pretrained video diffusion models. The resulting representation supports sparse local 3D motion handles, coordinated camera–object choreography, and piecewise-rigid approximations of non-rigid motion without requiring physics simulators or category-specific priors. To preserve the generative prior of a pretrained video model, we freeze a Wan-based diffusion backbone and condition it through lightweight adaptation layers driven by the proposed correspondence representation. Experiments demonstrate coherent camera-relative motion, improved geometric consistency under viewpoint changes, and strong controllability across diverse real-world scenes.

Contributions

  1. A dense correspondence-map representation that encodes point identity and position in a shared world coordinate system, as rasterized maps compatible with pretrained video VAEs.
  2. A local 3D motion-handle formulation that approximates non-rigid motion through independently controlled local rigid regions, without a simulator or a category-specific prior.
  3. A world-space authoring interface for joint control of camera trajectories and local object motion from a single image.

Method

  1. We estimate depth and a dynamic mask for the input image and back-project its pixels into a colored point cloud, using the first camera as the world frame.
  2. The artist selects local foreground regions as motion handles in a 3D tool such as Blender, then authors their trajectories and the camera path.
  3. For every frame we project the static background and the moved handles into three maps: background XYZ, foreground XYZ, and foreground identity, each with a validity mask.
  4. A frozen Wan VAE encodes the maps. A trainable side branch, convolutional projections between branches, and LoRA adapters in the main branch condition the frozen diffusion model on them.

Only the side branch, the cross-branch projections, and the LoRA adapters are trained. The side branch runs at half the spatial resolution of the main branch and adds its features to the main branch through residual connections.

Correspondence maps

Both XYZ maps are normalized once, using the bounding-box center o and half-range σ of the first-frame scene. The normalized X, Y, and Z coordinates are stored as red, green, and blue, with the same normalization in every frame:

RGB = ½ [(p − o) / σ + 1]

A pixel's location in the map tells the model where a point appears in the image, and its color tells the model where that point is in the world. A moving point changes its XYZ color, so a separate identity map gives each handle a fixed color across frames and viewpoints.

Limitations

A single view gives incomplete geometry. Errors in monocular depth and surfaces hidden in the input image can cause geometric inconsistencies, especially under large viewpoint changes, reflections, and thin structures.

Handles move as locally rigid groups. They suit local motion better than tearing, fracture, or fluids, and fine deformation may need many handles, which adds authoring work.

Independent trajectories do not enforce physical or causal constraints. The XYZ maps also use a fixed first-frame normalization range, which large edits can exceed.
